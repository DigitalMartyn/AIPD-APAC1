## Background

*Describe what the project is about, its context and background, and why it is being carried out.*

### What the course requires

You will define, design, and present a complete end-to-end AI product concept, delivered as a
portfolio-ready case study. The project runs alongside the taught programme, with each module
feeding directly into the next stage of your work.

### What you need to write

* The sector and the situation. What industry are you designing for, and what is happening in it
  right now that makes this problem worth solving?
* The problem, stated without the solution in it. For example, "Warehouse supervisors cannot tell
  which of 40 open exceptions actually needs them today," rather than "Warehouse supervisors need
  an AI dashboard."
* Who currently owns this problem and how it gets handled today, such as a spreadsheet, a phone
  call, a person's memory, or nothing at all. The status quo is your real competitor.
* Why AI, specifically. Course module 01 covers when AI adds value and when it does not. Be honest:
  if a rules engine, a better form, or removing a step would fix it, say so and pick a different
  problem. A concept that survives this question is a much stronger case study than one that
  dodges it.
* Where this sits in a wider journey. Is this a moment inside a longer workflow? Link or sketch that
  journey so the scope of your piece is clear.

## Objective

*Describe the ideal future state once this project is finished.*

### Course-level objective

Produce a case study that demonstrates strategic AI product thinking: problem framing, system
behaviour, cross-functional collaboration, ethical reasoning, and measurable outcomes, at a
standard you would put in front of a hiring manager or a senior stakeholder.

### Product-level objective

* The ideal future state. Describe the day in the life of your user *after* your product exists.
  Write one paragraph in the present tense without a feature list.
* Benefit to the business. Whose budget pays for this and what do they get? This might be cost
  avoided, revenue enabled, risk reduced, or capacity released. Name the mechanism, not only the
  direction.
* Benefit to the user. This might be time returned, anxiety removed, confidence gained, or a
  decision made better. If the business benefit and user benefit are in tension, as they often are
  with AI products, say so now rather than discovering it in the ethics module.
* How this ladders up. State the fictional or real company strategy your product serves. You are
  practising the argument you will need to make in a real product review.

## Key results and success criteria

*How will you know the project was successful?*

Define two layers. Both are assessed.

### Product success metrics

Define how you would know the product worked if it shipped. Cover at least the following metrics.

| Metric type | What to define | Example |
|-------------|----------------|---------|
| Adoption | Who starts using it, and how you measure meaningful use rather than a click | Percentage of eligible users completing a task with the feature at least three times per month |
| Task outcome | The user's job, done better | Median time to resolve an exception falls from 18 minutes to under 6 minutes |
| Trust and reliance | Whether users trust it appropriately, including whether they over-trust it | Override rate stays between 5% and 20%; sustained 0% means users have stopped checking |
| Model or system quality | The quality bar the experience depends on, and what happens below it | A confidence threshold below which the system defers to a human |
| Business | The commercial or operational result | Cost per case handled or retention of the paying account |
| Guardrail | The thing you must not break while improving the other metrics | Escalation-to-human rate, complaint volume, or fairness gap between user groups |

Set a **baseline**, describing what today looks like, and a **target** for each metric. Estimates are
acceptable when you state your assumption and explain how you would validate it.

### Project success criteria

Use these criteria to determine whether your case study landed:

* All five assignments are submitted, each incorporating feedback from the previous one.
* A reviewer who has never heard your idea can follow the story end to end without you narrating it.
* Your ethical reasoning is specific to your product, not generic AI safety language.
* The final artefact is genuinely portfolio-ready. You would send the link tomorrow.

## Target audience

*Describe the target audience for this project.*

You define the audience. Meet the following requirements:

* Identify one primary end user, described sharply enough that you would recognise them. Include
  their role, context of use, device, environment, time pressure, domain expertise, and AI literacy.
  AI literacy drives many of your interface decisions.
* Identify secondary users and affected parties. Who else touches this system, such as reviewers,
  approvers, or administrators? Critically for AI products, who is affected by the output without
  ever using the interface? This might be the applicant being scored, patient being triaged, or
  driver being routed. They are part of your audience whether or not they have a login.
* Identify edge users. Who does the model likely serve worst, such as non-native speakers, users
  with atypical histories, users with low data footprints, or users with accessibility needs?
  Identify them early; module 10 will hold you to it.
* Build personas or proto-personas from whatever real input you can get: interviews, forum posts,
  reviews, or colleagues in the sector. If you use AI to generate or synthesise personas, disclose
  that and explain how you sanity-checked them.

## Scope

### What is in scope

Every project must cover, at minimum:

1. Problem framing: the problem, target user, and role of AI (Assignment 1, Lessons 1 to 3).
2. Product definition: one or two workshop artefacts, a product proposition, and a focused rapid
  prototype (Assignment 2, Lessons 4 to 6).
3. Testing and experience design: a test plan, evidence, end-to-end flow, key screens, uncertainty
  and failure states, and the next iteration (Assignment 3, Lessons 7 to 9).
4. Learning and responsibility: an updated prototype, ethical assessment, learning plan,
  guardrails, and conversational rationale where relevant (Assignment 4, Lessons 10 and 11).
5. The case study narrative: the complete story, presented (Assignment 5, Lessons 12 and 13).

### What might be in scope

Include these only if they strengthen your argument and you have the time. Note the condition
alongside each item.

* Live user testing with three to five real users, if you can recruit them. This is strongly
  encouraged.
* Conversational or voice interface design, if your product is genuinely better as zero-UI
  (module 11).
* A working AI-powered demo through Lovable, Vercel, or a similar service, if it demonstrates
  something a static prototype cannot.
* Multi-platform or responsive coverage, only if the use context demands it.
* Detailed model evaluation or prompt architecture, if it is the crux of the experience.
* A service blueprint or operating model for the human side of the workflow, if humans in the loop
  are central.
* A business model, pricing, or go-to-market plan, if the commercial argument is your strongest
  angle.

### What is not in scope

* Building production software. This is a design case study; prototypes are prototypes.
* Training or fine-tuning your own model. Design around realistic capabilities of existing models.
* Guaranteed technical accuracy of model performance figures. Reason from published capabilities
  and state your assumptions.
* A full design system from scratch. Use or adapt an existing kit and spend your time on AI-specific
  patterns.
* Real user data. Use synthetic or anonymised data throughout. Never put identifiable personal
  information into a prototype or public case study.
* Every feature of the wider platform. Scope to one coherent journey. Depth beats breadth in a
  portfolio.
* Extending past 14 Oct. The presentation date is fixed.

> [!NOTE]
> These are guides, not walls. If your research produces a strong reason to challenge one, bring
> the rationale to a crit. That argument is itself good material for the case study.

## Dependencies

* Instructor feedback at each assignment checkpoint. The next stage builds on the previous one, so
  a late submission compounds.
* Access to users or domain experts in your chosen sector. This is the single most common blocker.
  Start recruiting in week one and prepare a fallback through desk research, published studies,
  community forums, or review mining.
* Tool access: Figma and FigJam for design and workshops; Claude, ChatGPT, or Copilot for synthesis
  and ideation; Lovable or Vercel for an optional functional demo. Set up accounts before
  Assignment 1.
* Domain knowledge in your sector, particularly the regulatory context if you choose health,
  finance, legal, education, or anything involving children.
* Peer availability for crits and testing.
* Your own time: roughly four to six hours a week outside class, front-loaded around assignment
  weeks.

## Risks

| Risk | Impact | Mitigation |
|------|--------|------------|
| Solution-first thinking: you picked the AI feature before the problem | The whole case study rests on a weak premise and reviewers see it immediately | Complete the "why AI" test in module 01 honestly. Be willing to change your idea after Assignment 1 |
| Scope creep into a whole platform | Nothing is finished to a portfolio standard | Lock one core journey by Assignment 2. Park everything else in a future scope section |
| No access to real users | Unvalidated assumptions throughout | Recruit in week one; fall back to proxy research and label it as such |
| Sector chosen for novelty, not knowledge | You cannot answer basic domain questions in the final review | Pick a sector you can discuss credibly or invest early in a domain contact |
| Ethics treated as generic language | The most scrutinised part of an AI case study reads as boilerplate | Apply the Lesson 10 assessment to the specific product and sector in Assignment 4 |
| Designing the happy path only | AI products fail in the unhappy path, which reviewers probe | Design uncertain, wrong, and escalation states as first-class work in Assignment 3 |
| Over-reliance on AI tooling for your own thinking | Generic output with no distinct point of view | Use AI to accelerate synthesis, not to make the calls. Keep a decision log in your own words |
| Missing the 14 Oct presentation | You present incomplete work and there is no second slot | Work to the checkpoint dates, not the final date |
| Prototype demo fails live | Lost time and composure in the final review | Record a backup walkthrough video before presentation day |

The final case study should contain:

* Problem, context, and sector
* Target user and affected parties
* Why AI is appropriate and which capability you are using

## Ways of working

### Team routines

* Live sessions take place on Mondays and Wednesdays at 6:30 PM AEST, including roughly 20 minutes
  of questions and answers at the end. Come with a specific question, not a general one.
* Run one-week personal sprints aligned with the class schedule. Each Monday, decide the one thing
  that must be true by Wednesday.
* Organise at least three peer crits: after Assignments 1 and 3, and before the final presentation.
  Whoever calls the crit brings a specific question and a time limit.
* Run a 15-minute solo retrospective after each assignment. Ask what changed in your thinking, what
  you avoided, and what the weakest part is right now.
* Keep one decision log that captures every significant decision, the alternatives considered, and
  why you chose. It becomes the spine of your case study and prevents you from having to reconstruct
  your reasoning in October.

### Communication

* Use live sessions and questions and answers for direction and critique from the instructor.
* Use the cohort channel for peer crits, recruiting test participants, and sharing resources.
* Communicate asynchronously with domain contacts by email or whatever they prefer. Keep it short
  and specific.
* Communicate with your future self through the decision log. Write it as if handing over to a
  colleague.

### Design, collaboration, and documentation tools

| Purpose | Tools |
|---------|-------|
| Design and prototyping | Figma |
| Workshops, synthesis, and flows | FigJam |
| Functional AI demo (optional) | Lovable, Vercel |
| Research synthesis, ideation, and drafting | Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot |
| Case study publication | Figma, Notion, personal site, or your portfolio platform of choice |

Document as you go and in the open. Screenshot the messy middle: rejected directions, a flow you
scrapped, or the ethical objection that changed your design. Portfolio reviewers hire for
reasoning, and the reasoning is only visible if you captured it while it was happening.

### Shared data

* Keep one project folder containing this brief, research notes, decision log, Figma files,
  prototype links, and assignment submissions.
* Name and version files consistently. You will be looking for "the version before the one where I
  changed the confidence threshold."
* Confirm sharing permissions on Figma links before every submission. A broken link reads as a
  missed deadline.
* Never store identifiable personal data from research participants. Anonymise it at the point of
  capture.

## Additional notes

### Choosing your sector

The best capstones usually come from someone's actual working life: a process they have watched
fail or a decision they have had to make with bad information. Novelty is not an advantage;
credibility is. You will be asked domain questions in the final review.

### Using AI to make this project

You are designing AI products while using AI tools, which is the actual job. Use them for
synthesis, alternatives, critique, and drafting. Do not outsource the judgement calls. The point
of difference in your case study is your point of view, and that has to be yours.

### Designing the unhappy path

With conventional software, the design job is mostly the happy path. With AI products, the
interesting work is what happens when the system is uncertain, wrong, biased, slow, or used for
something you did not anticipate. Reviewers know this. Spend your time there.

### Presenting

Module 12 covers storytelling for complex AI systems, and module 13 is your presentation. Rehearse
out loud at least twice. Ten minutes disappears faster than you think, and the concept you have
lived with for seven weeks is completely new to everyone listening.
